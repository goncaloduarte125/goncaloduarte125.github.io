Sparsity matters

Sparsity is a known fact about LLMs and something people have thought about for a long time. Here is Dario Amodei in the famous essay “The Big Blob of Compute Hypothesis” (2017):

“Note that none of this implies that scaling up today’s exact algorithms will lead to AGI. It does suggest that any algorithmic changes will likely be simple things that exploit symmetries or sparsity (good candidates might be memory-based models, or neural nets that grow in size over the course of training), or innovations in the training process, and that scale will be a huge part of the story.”

Attention maps are empirically very concentrated, meaning that most of the probability mass is concentrated in a small subset of keys. As the demand for ever larger context windows increases, decoding becomes bandwidth constrained as a result of the size of the KV Cache. In practice this means that we end up moving gigabytes of memory to multiply by roughly nothing. In order to deal with this, existing approaches either compress the KV cache and evict a subset of tokens, or select a subset of the KV cache to attend to for each query. The first family of methods risks losing information that might become relevant at later steps. For the second family, methods use a fixed budget, either for tokens or probability mass. The right budget varies across heads, layers and queries so any fixed choice risks wasting bandwidth or dropping relevant tokens.

With this in mind, we have seen a great deal of work on methods that take advantage of sparsity at different levels with efficiency in mind:

  • KV-cache compression or eviction risk: losing information that is needed at later steps
  • Learned top-k sparsity : better, but still fixed.
  • KV-cache selection: determine a candidate set using an importance proxy and decode using that set only

Lets go back to the attention formula

\[z_j = \frac{q^\top k_j}{\sqrt{d}}, \qquad p = \operatorname{softmax}(z), \qquad o = \sum_j p_j v_j.\]

This means that the output is a probability weighted sum of the values, as we use Softmax to turn the scores into a probability distribution. Is there a way that we can get sparsity from the probability map itself? Yes—$\alpha$-entmax.

Sparsity from the probability map

The output is a probability-weighted sum of the values, with softmax as the probability map that turns scores into probabilities. It can be written as a regularized argmax over the probability simplex $\Delta$, with Shannon entropy $H^S(\mathbf p) = -\sum_j p_j \log p_j$ as the regularizer:

\[\operatorname{softmax}(\mathbf z) = \arg\max_{\mathbf p \in \Delta} \; \mathbf p^\top \mathbf z + H^{S}(\mathbf p).\]

Swapping Shannon entropy for Tsallis entropy gives $\alpha$-entmax:

\[\alpha\text{-entmax}(\mathbf z) = \arg\max_{\mathbf p \in \Delta} \; \mathbf p^\top \mathbf z + \tfrac{1}{\alpha(\alpha-1)}\textstyle\sum_j (p_j - p_j^\alpha).\]

Its solution has a closed form, and the key object in it is the threshold $\tau$:

\[\alpha\text{-entmax}(\mathbf z)_j = \big[(\alpha-1) z_j - \tau\big]_+^{1/(\alpha-1)}, \qquad \tau \text{ chosen so that } \textstyle\sum_j p_j = 1 .\]

As $\alpha \to 1$ we recover softmax, and $\alpha = 2$ gives sparsemax. For any $\alpha > 1$, every key whose score falls below the threshold gets exactly zero probability.

Entmax plays well with top-k

Entmax can assign exact zeros, avoiding a dense tail. Does this mean that entmax better suited to existing sparse methods? Our experiments on a model pair where only the probability map differs indicate so. Note that the models are also trained with the different probability maps so they learn different Q/K geometries which in turn can also affect how they behave under different decoding strategies so it’s not as simple as “no zeros” -> less error. However, we can make it more concrete:

Consider a selection strategy that keeps a subset $\mathcal{I}_{\text{keep}} \subseteq [n]$ and drops its complement $\mathcal{I}_{\text{drop}} = [n] \setminus \mathcal{I}_{\text{keep}}$. Let

\[\delta = \sum_{i \in \mathcal{I}_{\text{drop}}} p_i\]

denote the dropped probability mass. The truncated and renormalized attention distribution is

\[\tilde{p}_i = \begin{cases} p_i / (1 - \delta), & i \in \mathcal{I}_{\text{keep}}, \\ 0, & i \in \mathcal{I}_{\text{drop}}. \end{cases}\]

The full and approximate attention outputs are

\[\mathbf{o} = \sum_{i=1}^{n} p_i \mathbf{v}_i \qquad \text{and} \qquad \tilde{\mathbf{o}} = \sum_{i=1}^{n} \tilde{p}_i \mathbf{v}_i .\]

Proposition (Approximation error under truncation). Let $\delta > 0$. If $\lVert \mathbf{v}_i \rVert_2 \le B$ for all $i$, then

\[\lVert \mathbf{o} - \tilde{\mathbf{o}} \rVert_2 \le 2B\delta .\]

Moreover, the constant is tight: equality can be attained with two tokens whose value vectors have norm $B$ and point in opposite directions.

The proof is given in the appendix of the paper. The proposition shows that truncated attention error is controlled by the mass assigned to discarded tokens. The identity of the dropped tokens matters only through their total probability mass. Thus, a sparse decoding method should select candidate tokens that make $\delta$ small.

Case Study: Quest

We use query-aware page scoring in the spirit of Quest on top of a paged KV Cache. Each page stores lightweight metadata that is then leveraged to compute a cheap relevance proxy (page criticality in Quest).

Concretely, for each page $p$, we store coordinate-wise minimum and maximum key vectors:

\[\mathbf{k}_{\min}^{(p)} = \min_{j \in \mathcal{P}_p} \mathbf{k}_j, \qquad \mathbf{k}_{\max}^{(p)} = \max_{j \in \mathcal{P}_p} \mathbf{k}_j.\]

The min and max are taken element-wise. Given a query $\mathbf{q}$, the maximum possible score of any token in page $p$ is upper-bounded by

\[\bar{s}_{\mathrm{box}}^{(p)}(\mathbf{q}) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{d}} \sum_{i=1}^{d} \max\!\left(q_i k_{\min,i}^{(p)}, q_i k_{\max,i}^{(p)}\right).\]

Recall the optimization problem

What do we get when we apply entmax? Among other things, we get the support size. This means that we can use to set the budget, and in the same way Quest computes a relevant proxy and then performs top-k, by approximating entmax, we get an adaptive budget and thus can fetch only the scores that we estimate to be above the threshold!

\[\left|\operatorname{supp}(\mathbf{p})\right| = \#\left\{j : (\alpha - 1)z_j > \tau\right\}.\]

By storing the page moments, we can solve for $\hat{\tau}$ using a mixture of Gaussians.

Solving for the distributional threshold

If we know $\tau$, we know which keys matter and how many there are. This turns sparse decoding into support recovery: whenever the set of tokens we load contains the support, the sparse output is exactly the full-cache output.

The catch is that $\tau$ depends on all the scores, and those are what we want to avoid computing. EntmaxKV estimates $\tau$ from page statistics instead. Alongside the min/max keys, each page also stores the coordinate-wise mean and standard deviation of its keys. For a query $\mathbf q$, we model the score of a random token in page $p$ as a Gaussian $S^{(p)} \sim \mathcal N(\mu_p, \sigma_p^2)$, where

\[\mu_p = \frac{\mathbf q^\top \mathbf k_{\mathrm{avg}}^{(p)}}{\sqrt d}, \qquad \sigma_p^2 = \frac{1}{d}\sum_{i=1}^d q_i^2 \big(k_{\mathrm{std},i}^{(p)}\big)^2 .\]

Writing

\[g_\alpha(z; \tau) = \left[(\alpha - 1)z - \tau\right]_+^{1/(\alpha - 1)}\]

for the entmax probability of a single score, we replace the exact normalization $\sum_j g_\alpha(z_j; \tau) = 1$ with its expectation under the page Gaussians:

\[\sum_{p=1}^{M} |\mathcal{P}_p| \, \mathbb{E}\!\left[g_\alpha\!\left(S^{(p)}; \hat{\tau}\right)\right] = 1 .\]

We solve this equation with a small fixed number of Newton or Halley iterations over $\tau$. Each iteration has constant cost per page because each page contributes only through $\mu_p$, $\sigma_p$, and evaluations of $\phi$ and $\Phi$. Importantly, this step does not require loading the full KV cache.

Tau tells us how much to read

In EntmaxKV, we formulate sparse decoding as support recovery and show that it can be exact whenever the candidate set contains the support. In doing this, we show that entmax is better suited to existing top-k approaches as it can generate absolute zeros in the probability distribution, meaning that it has no dense tail. But more importantly, by noting that in entmax the support size of the distribution is an output of the mapping that transforms the scores into a distribution, and focusing on $\tau$, the distributional threshold, we can design a method inherently adaptive to the underlying problem.

Making it fast

Practical speedup gains required more than estimating the support and constructing a page mask. Just as hardware co-design can yield drastic speedups as in FlashAttention FlashAttention, taking into account the underlying optimization problem being solved when writing our decode algorithm and the Triton GPU Kernels enables an implementation that is faster than Softmax Attention at long-context, and at the same time uses a budget that adapts to the current query.

At short contexts the selection overhead dominates. The payoff grows with length: at 1M tokens, the Gaussian selector runs in 3.6 ms, which is 2.88× faster than softmax FlashDecoding, 5.5× faster than full entmax, and about 2× faster than both top-k variants.

Closing thoughts

This work led to the publication of EntmaxKV: Support-Aware Decoding for Entmax Attention, accepted at the Long Context Foundation Models workshop at NeurIPS 2026. The paper presents an entmax-native sparse decoding framework for long-context inference, combining query-aware page scoring, support-aware candidate selection, and sparse entmax attention for autoregressive decoding.

We showed that truncation error is controlled by the dropped probability mass $\delta$, and that this error vanishes when the entmax support is recovered. We also introduced a Gaussian-aware entmax selector that estimates the entmax threshold from page-level statistics, allowing the candidate budget to adapt to the score distribution. Empirically, EntmaxKV retains more support tokens, drops less probability mass, and achieves lower output error than softmax-based sparse decoding at matched KV budgets, while improving decoding efficiency over full entmax and dense attention baselines.

For more details you can read it on arXiv.

Related work

EntmaxKV builds on a long line of work on sparse probability maps and the tools to make them practical.

Sparse probability maps. Sparsemax replaced softmax with a Euclidean projection onto the simplex, showing that attention can assign exact zeros and still be trained end to end. A general regularized framework for sparse and structured attention followed, and $\alpha$-entmax interpolates between softmax and sparsemax through Tsallis entropy.

Fenchel-Young losses. Fenchel-Young losses pair each regularized probability map with a matching convex loss. This gives a single view of softmax/cross-entropy, sparsemax and entmax, and a principled way to train models that output sparse distributions.

Entmax attention. Adaptively sparse transformers brought entmax into self-attention and learned $\alpha$ per head, so each head can decide how sparse to be. ASEntmax adds learnable, input-dependent temperatures and shows that sparse attention helps length generalization by countering attention dispersion, representational collapse and over-squashing.

Efficient entmax kernels. Entmax was long held back by speed. AdaSplash introduced FlashAttention-style GPU kernels for $\alpha$-entmax that skip blocks that are entirely zero. AdaSplash-2 made training faster still with histogram-based threshold initialization and better block skipping, and showed strong long-context results. The entmax and softmax checkpoints used in this post come from that work. Both make entmax practical during training, but decoding with them still reads the full KV cache. EntmaxKV is complementary: it uses entmax sparsity during autoregressive decoding, where the bottleneck is reading the KV cache.
